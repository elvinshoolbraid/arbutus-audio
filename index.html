Mission Log Updates

The Arbutus-1: A music studio in your backpack.

A portable synthesizer, sampler and four-track recorder. Easy to start, impossible to outgrow, designed to be open.

The short version

Arbutus-1 is a portable synthesizer, sampler and four-track recorder: a two-octave keyboard, four knobs, a small screen, a microphone, a speaker and a battery, in a printed shell about the size of a paperback. You can make a finished piece of music on it, start to end, with nothing else.

I'm building it because making music should be approachable, and the tools mostly aren't. The device that does this best, the OP-1 Field, costs about $2,800 CAD, is glued shut, and runs software nobody outside the company can change. Cheaper devices drop the multitrack recording that makes it worth owning. Desktop software like Ableton is a spaceship cockpit with no direction in it. None of that is a technical necessity; it is a set of choices, and I think a small company can make different ones.

  • Under $500 CAD

    Off-the-shelf compute, small-batch circuit boards, a 3D-printed enclosure made close to the customer, sold direct. Most of the price of the incumbents is not parts.

  • Made to be opened

    Bolts, not glue. Print files, board files and repair guides published. Spare parts sold at fair prices. A device you can fix is a device that stays out of the landfill.

  • Open hardware and software

    Synth engines, effects and sequencers are plugins. Anyone can write one and share it, and the best community work can be incorporated into the base product.

  • Approachable by design

    The biggest barrier is intimidation. So the functions are printed on the keys, the software says what it is doing, and holding HELP explains whatever you are touching. A phone app with tutorial modes, and repair guides you can actually follow. Function first, without giving up the form.

Where it stands: one fully validated circuit board, a look-alike model, and a playable emulator of the operating system. The next board is being designed now. I'm one person, a mechanical engineering student at the University of Victoria, with an industrial designer as a mentor and AI coding tools. The log below is the whole story, newest first.

September 2026Software

ArbutusOS, running on a laptop

The emulator. The screen is live, and the keys and knobs in the photo are clickable.

The next circuit board will take weeks to design, order and bring up, and I wanted something people could play at Hollyhock this week. So the software went first. This is ArbutusOS running on my MacBook, drawn inside the photo of the look-alike model. The keys and knobs in the photo respond to the mouse, and a USB MIDI keyboard plays the synth.

What's in it: a SYNTH page with several engines, a DRUM kit, a four-track TRACK page with tape-style recording, lift and drop, slicing and looping, a MIXER, a step sequencer, TEMPO with tap and click, live microphone sampling on INPUT, a HELP key that explains whatever you're holding, and FILE for saving projects. The audio engine is a native library the real device will run too, so this is not a mock-up of the software. It is the software, with around 280 automated tests and a screenshot harness that checks each page against a reference image.

The method was new for me. I had Claude interview me, starting from the repository and the design documents, until every open question about the software had an answer, and that became the spec. Claude Opus then built the emulator over about ten days of sessions. What one person can build in a month has changed a lot in a year.

September 2026Industrial design

The look-alike

Three PETG colours: beige top, bark-orange base, forest-green labels and one knob.

Jacks and USB on the right end. The rear is taller to hold the batteries.

Top-down, as photographed for the emulator. Fifty-two keys on a uniform 16 mm grid.

Before the emulator, half a week on a model that looks like the product and does nothing. Three colours of Bambu Lab PETG that read as arbutus: bark, wood and leaf. Fifty-two keys on a uniform 16 mm grid, four encoders, and a screen cutout at the real display size.

The keycaps are printed in the same filaments as the chassis with a 0.2 mm nozzle, with the label printed into the cap as a second colour. That was the real experiment. It worked far better than any other way I have found to label keys, which means pilot production can use printed caps, and probably the long run too. Still untested: the interlock between switch and keycap.

What it settled: I like the layout and the count of controls enough to commit to them. The next board can use these key positions as its footprint, and the chassis only needs to change in depth once the real component heights are known. It also brought back a good deal of confidence after a stalled summer.

August 2026Process

One question per prototype

Over the summer the project stalled. The second circuit board had grown into an attempt to answer everything at once: final component selection, a redone schematic, a split into several boards with untested ribbon connectors, a chassis designed around components I had never held, and a key layout for a user interface I had never used. Those variables all depend on each other, so no single build could converge on all of them, and there was not enough room in my head to solve them together. For a while I read that as a character problem. It was a scoping problem.

Paul, my mentor, worked on Tickle Me Elmo at Mattel and pointed out that it went through nine prototypes. So I wrote out a ladder where each build answers exactly one question and freezes everything else as an assumption of record.

  1. P0Loose dev boards on a bench. Can these parts talk at all?Done
  2. P1One big validation board. Does the whole signal chain run?Done
  3. P2Look-alike shell plus laptop emulator, as separate objects. Does the product read as desirable, and does the interface land in someone else's hands?Done
  4. P3A second monolithic board with all the new parts. Is the key layout right, and do the new components work?Next
  5. P4Printed shell with dummy boards at true thickness. Does the stack physically assemble?After P3
  6. P5The split-board build in a real chassis. Does the integrated product work as one unit?Parked
  7. P6+Engineering, design and production validation builds, the standard gates before manufacturing.Later

P3 is deliberately one board again, a mule. It carries the new parts: a three-cell battery management circuit with 21700 clips, a thinner Kailh key switch that takes about 6 mm off the device, MEMS microphones, a motion sensor, and ribbon connectors placed only so they can be tested. It also gets a 40-pin header so a Raspberry Pi Zero 2 or Pi 3 can be tried in place of the Compute Module 4. Early emulator measurements suggest the smaller computer may be enough, which could take about $100 off the bill of materials.

The lesson I am trying to keep: when work stalls, ask what this prototype is actually trying to answer before asking whether to push harder.

March to April 2026Electronics

Splitting the board

The encoder board's schematic for revision two, with every fix from the first board marked in red.

One connector type for every board-to-board link, to keep the part count down.

The interaction matrix: what every key does on every page. The real DNA of the software.

With the first board proven, I started revision two as separate boards: compute, input and output, encoder, keyboard and battery, joined by 20-pin ribbon cables, each its own KiCad project so JLCPCB can build them separately. I found a 21700 battery clip that their assembly line can place by machine, and decided to design the battery management circuit onto that board rather than bolting a bought module in.

I also started an interaction matrix, a spreadsheet of what every key does on every page, because software cannot be built from a vague description, by me or by anyone I hand it to. By late April the compute board schematic was done, the input and battery boards were drafted, and there was a set of mechanical design documents covering fasteners, keycap construction, a print cost model and chassis geometry.

Then the summer happened. The post above this one is what came out of it.

March 2026Electronics

Everything on the board works

Eleven wires carry the display signals to the spare board's connector, reordered to undo a mirrored footprint.

ArbutusOS v0.1, a diagnostic screen that shows every key press and knob turn.

A 440 Hz tone sent out the headphone jack and recorded back through line-in.

The display finally came up. Soldering straight to the LCD's ribbon pads was unreliable, so I ran eleven wires to the display connector on the spare board instead, reordered to undo a mirrored connector in my design. With that, a diagnostic version of the operating system showed every key press and knob turn on screen.

Line-in and headphone-out were tested with a 440 Hz round trip. The spectrum shows harmonic distortion from gain staging, not digital noise leaking into the audio path, so the analog layout can stay as it is.

That closed electrical validation. Power, the Compute Module, the audio codec, the keyboard controller, the encoders and the display were all verified on the first board.

February 2026Electronics

Flashing the keyboard chip

Six programming wires on pads much smaller than I had pictured when I drew them.

Second board wired up in case the first chip was dead, with a logic analyser on the programming lines.

An Arduino injecting a clock. The chip expected an external crystal that was never fitted.

After the fix: tester key switches, the display on red wires, and the Compute Module doing the programming.

The ATmega microcontroller that scans the keyboard would not accept firmware. It turned out to be two faults at once: the chip was waiting for an external crystal I had not fitted, and the programmer was releasing the reset line too late to matter. Finding that took an Arduino injecting a clock signal, a second board wired up in case the first chip was dead, a logic analyser on the programming lines, and finally the Compute Module on the board itself doing the programming through 0.1 mm wires.

Once it took, the encoders and the first key switches read correctly. Two lessons went in the log. Don't decide a chip is dead until you can prove it. And when an AI assistant contradicts itself on a datasheet detail, make it go and check, because that one cost a week.

February 2026Software, mechanical

First interface on a screen, and a two-piece shell

The first proof-of-concept interface, running on the Mac. Four parameters on the four knobs.

Shift and a number opens the engine picker.

A new shell in two pieces and six bolts.

The large nozzle used here left a rougher finish than PETG deserves. Later prints went back to a fine nozzle.

Meanwhile, the programming pads that would take the rest of the month.

While the keyboard chip was being fought with, I had Claude Code build a proof of concept of the software: a synth page with four parameters on the four knobs, an engine picker, and sound out, running on the Mac through a thin emulation layer. It proved that the libraries and architecture we had picked would work before anything ran on the device.

Separately, a new enclosure printed in two pieces with six bolts, a big reduction from the previous shell, to find out how simple assembly could get. It still needed work on rigidity and on the surface finish from the large nozzle, but the direction held.

January 2026Electronics

The board arrives

Back from JLCPCB with the small parts placed. Encoders, jacks and key switches are mine to add.

The micro-soldering setup: microscope camera, hot air, paste, and a monitor to work from.

Three 21700 cells and a management board. The pack read the right voltage first time.

A 0.1 mm wire onto one pin of the Compute Module connector, for a connection the schematic missed.

The USB connector had a short under it that would not clear, so a cable was spliced onto the pads instead.

The validation board came back over the holidays with most of the parts placed. I set up a microscope camera, hot air and paste, learned micro-soldering from a colleague who does it for a living, and fitted the two 100-pin connectors for the Compute Module 4. Twice, because one lifted off with the module and the other took a drop of solder somewhere it should not be.

Three 21700 cells and a management board made the pack, and the 5 V and 3.3 V rails measured right. The Compute Module booted after a missing connection was bridged with 0.1 mm wire and a backwards diode was turned around. USB needed a cable spliced straight onto the pads. By the end of the month the audio codec was answering over I2C, with Claude Code diagnosing most of the problems from the terminal.

Power converters on the left, the audio codec and jacks on the right, test pads everywhere.

Mid-migration from EasyEDA to KiCad.

The first board's job is to test the schematic, not to fit the product. So: one big board, no attention to where anything sits in a chassis, larger passive components so I can rework them by hand, test pads on every important trace, ground planes on both signal layers with stitching vias. I migrated from EasyEDA to KiCad on the way, following Phil's Lab's layout course, and ordered from JLCPCB in mid-December.

A size-and-shape model, printed to take to Discover Tectoria, Victoria's technology expo: a 25 cm shell with the inclined display and a printed lattice inside. Talking to strangers about the project for a day produced the first mailing list, twelve people, and a few software contacts.

It was also the fortnight I realised I had been massively overthinking the first circuit board. It did not need to pass emissions tests, survive static discharge, or be shaped like the product yet. A friend pointed that out, and layout went quickly afterwards.

October 2025Electronics

Multi-board, and the arbutus branch

The keyboard controller with its new IO expander and board-to-board connectors.

The schematic was reorganised into four boards joined by a single type of 20-pin ribbon connector, so there is one connector part to stock. A conflict surfaced: the external USB port and the keyboard controller both wanted the Compute Module's one USB pair. The fix was a serial link for the controller and an IO expander to free up its pins.

A new mock-up got an inclined display and the arbutus branch that became the logo. I also built a 90-second pitch for the university's PitchIt competition, and made a deliberate decision to under-do circuit protection and interference filtering on the first version, since none of it affects whether the prototype works.

The start of an entrepreneurial co-op term at the University of Victoria, and the project on paper: a Raspberry Pi Compute Module 4, a WM8960 audio codec, an ATmega32U4 keyboard controller, a small LCD, three 21700 cells. Schematics in EasyEDA for audio, display, power and USB, then the keyboard matrix and encoders, chosen from parts JLCPCB can place so the boards arrive mostly assembled. A not-to-scale model to try aesthetics.

Customer interviews began with Paul and a friend. The conclusion I took from a founder's talk at the innovation centre stuck: nothing useful comes back from customers until there is a thing to hold. So the goal became a working prototype first, and everything else after.
